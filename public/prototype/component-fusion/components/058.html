Intro

The 0.3 seconds after a tap when nothing happens is longer than you think. People read that brief silence as “broken.”

So good transitions respond first and show the result later. When something moves the instant you press, waiting stops feeling like waiting.

Three principles

First, start where the finger landed. Second, move only what changes. Third, when stopping, overshoot slightly and settle back.

Keep just these three and a screen feels far less stiff. The third one in particular adds a sense of weight.

In practice

When a product flies into the cart icon, people follow the result with their eyes. It leads to fewer mistakes than a number quietly changing.

When the highlight slides between tabs, you can still tell where you came from. It keeps people from getting lost.

The same goes for deleting from a list. When the items below slowly close the gap, you naturally see what was removed.

Wrap-up

Transitions aren’t decoration but explanation — the shortest sentence for what went where.

Too long and it turns into waiting; too short and no one sees it. Most answers lie between 0.2 and 0.5 seconds.

And for people who prefer less motion, deliver the same information without it. That is part of a good transition too.
